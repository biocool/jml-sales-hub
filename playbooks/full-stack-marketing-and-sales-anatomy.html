How to read this book

Who it’s for

Start here if sales and marketing meetings sound like a string of familiar words with unfamiliar meanings. There are no prerequisites. Each article defines terms when they first appear and ties them to a decision you can observe or make.

Article shapes

Concepts explain one idea through a problem, analogy, numbered mechanism and consequences. Comparisons present real options and a selection rule. Skills give a repeatable work sequence and common mistakes. Patterns show checkpoints, ownership, and what evidence must pass through.

Short on time? Read Intent, inspect the hero plate, and skim the decision rule. Come back for the worked example and try it before revealing the answer.

One case throughout

Juniper Table is a fictional local restaurant. Harbor and Garden are separate locations. Leo can verify the Harbor operations; Maya is the owner. Juniper needs to help diners find information and reserve tables. JML needs to qualify an inquiry and deliver the agreed work. The book keeps the two scoreboards separate.

Follow the lime

Each article opens with a diagram built around the idea it explains. A booking path, a CRM record and a forecast each need a different shape. JML purple connects the visual language. A lime marker indicates the part you are inspecting, not success or an outcome. Select a number in the image or the matching button below it to see what that part does. More complex articles include a second plate for a failure mode, comparison or worked mechanism.

Learning path

  1. Read M1 through M3. Practice explaining a buyer, an offer and a funnel.
  2. Read M4 through M7. Draft a channel plan and verify the measurement path.
  3. Read S1 through S4. Rehearse a discovery call and route a record in the dispatch lab.
  4. Read S5 through S7. Review a scope with a mentor, then complete the capstone.

Find the right market

Research, segmentation & ICP

Intent

A market is a group with a similar problem. A segment is a narrower slice of it; an ideal customer profile (ICP) describes which businesses are the best fit. A persona describes an individual role inside one. Start with a problem and evidence, not with a list of demographics.

The problem

At Juniper Harbor, an owner sees an outdated menu link and assumes an agency should sell every restaurant a listing cleanup. But an observable symptom is not necessarily a market. An owner who fixed it last year, a new manager struggling every week, and a franchise operator without access may all show the same symptom and need entirely different things.

Good segmentation predicts differences in whether the problem recurs, who can act and why they would pay. Before picking restaurants by size, identify a trigger that changes their behavior: seasonal hours, frequent menu updates, or ownership changes. Separate the approver, the person living with the problem and whoever controls the account. Their motives and ability to act often diverge.

The solution

JML’s first segment: independently operated restaurants with one to three locations, a verified Google profile, and a manager who can confirm opening hours and booking links. Maya is the approving owner; Leo is an operator, not automatically the buyer. A chain whose corporate team controls all listings is out of scope for the pilot.

Real-world analogy

Think of triage at a repair shop: two cars make the same sound, but one needs a worn part replaced and another has a loose item in the trunk. A good segment identifies the common repair, not merely the sound.

How it works

  1. State an inclusion hypothesis as a behavior: managers change hours at least seasonally and must update more than one public destination.
  2. Choose pilot cases that let you compare the hypothesis with rivals: independent restaurants with and without frequent updates, plus a few comparable centrally managed locations.
  3. Ask for the last concrete event, not an opinion: when did hours change, what was updated, and which step took the longest? Write down exceptions and disagreements.
  4. Tag each account for problem recurrence, owner control, access readiness and willingness to take a scoped next step. Keep unknown separate from no.
  5. Choose the first segment based on repeatability and ability to fix the problem. Keep rejected accounts and the reason in the record so a new discovery can reopen the decision.

Worked example

Illustrative intake: of twelve restaurants, four had both recurring changes and local account control. Three of those four independently described confusion about holiday timing. Three other locations showed public discrepancies but could only request edits from corporate. That is a governance problem for a different buyer, not proof the original offer should quietly expand.

One independent restaurant fixed each issue in under five minutes and had no interest in outside help. Keep it in an observation log, not a lead pipeline. The apparent problem already has a good enough substitute.

When to use it

Use this process when creating an offer, entering a new neighborhood or abandoning a weak pipeline. Change one boundary at a time so you can tell whether the audience changes or only the evidence changes. Carry the strongest problem statement to positioning in M2; carry the best evidence of buyer intent to channel selection in M5.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Filters leads by ability to act, not just surface fit.
  • Tells a small team which cases not to pursue and why.

Limits

  • Interview results are biased toward people willing to talk and cannot size the market by themselves.
  • A narrow pilot gives faster learning but can miss a valuable buyer with a longer approval cycle.

Do the work

Write an evidence sheet: location, two public links, what visibly differs, date and timezone, who can verify it, and a reason to exclude. Interview the operator without suggesting an answer: “Walk me through what happens when someone tries to book Sunday dinner.”

Problem confirmation = interviews confirming the problem ÷ substantive interviews. Five out of eight is directional, not a market estimate; early interviewees may be biased.

Say it in a meeting

“We have five owners describing the same updating problem. Which accounts did we exclude, and what would change our mind?”

Try it

Draft an ICP in one sentence, with two inclusion criteria and one exclusion. What can you infer from Juniper Table’s Sunday hours showing 8 p.m. on Maps and 9 p.m. on its site?

Reveal model answer

Model: independent local restaurants with control of their profile and a recurring hours/menu update need; exclude locations where corporate must approve every change. The two pages conflict. They don’t tell us which is correct or whether anyone was lost.

Make the offer specific

Positioning, messaging & copy

Intent

Positioning explains why someone should consider you instead of an alternative. An offer is the concrete thing they get next, and a call to action (CTA) names what to do. A value proposition connects a buyer’s problem to a useful outcome and believable evidence.

The problem

Juniper Harbor’s draft says the agency delivers a business profile audit, listing consistency, conversion optimization and monthly support. Those are work categories. An owner reading a flyer between shifts wants to know whether it addresses a problem they recognize and what happens after they respond.

Positioning chooses the alternative against which the offer makes sense. A restaurant owner might compare the audit not to another agency but to asking Leo to look at the listing after closing. The offer should beat that alternative on something verifiable: a second set of eyes, a cross-surface check, or a prioritized list the team can use without hiring anybody.

The solution

“See where diners find different hours, menu links or booking instructions. Request a one-location discovery audit.” The evidence is a dated before-and-after customer journey, if authorized. The immediate outcome is clarity on what to fix, not a promise to rank first or gain revenue.

Real-world analogy

Think of a mechanic offering a brake inspection instead of selling a garage subscription. The inspection answers a practical question, has a boundary and leaves the customer free to decide on repairs.

How it works

  1. Record the buyer’s most likely alternative and one job they want done now, in language they use.
  2. Turn the promise into a deliverable: name what will be examined, what comes back, and what is expressly not included.
  3. Pair each substantive claim with the proof required to say it. A dated screenshot can prove what a person could see, not how many customers walked away.
  4. Write the next step as an action and set an expectation about the information required and what happens after submission.
  5. Hand the message to someone unlike the author; ask them to explain who it is for, what they receive and whether they are signing up for anything ongoing.

Worked example

Two illustrative messages test boundaries. A: “Find out what customers see before they book.” B: “Get a checklist of conflicting hours, menu and booking links for one location.” If A produces more visits but people expect a review of their full website, B may be the better promise even with fewer clicks.

A sample output might say: “Sunday hours differ across two public pages. Verify which closing time applies, then update the other source.” It shows the work without pretending the discrepancy caused losses or promising to edit without approval.

When to use it

Use narrower copy whenever evidence is weak or the buyer could reasonably mistake the offer for an ongoing commitment. Expand the offer only if repeated conversations support it and delivery capacity exists. In M3 the landing page should honor the exact promise; in M6 the claims checklist should reference this message brief.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • An explicit deliverable lets the buyer self-qualify and lets Sales validate what was promised.
  • Proof-led copy is easier for a client approver to approve and for a teammate to reuse.

Limits

  • A precise promise can attract fewer casual clicks and feel less dramatic than a broad claim.
  • If a substitute truly solves the job cheaply, rewriting the headline will not fix the value proposition.

Do the work

Write a one-page message brief: who it’s for, observed friction, desired change, exact deliverable, boundaries, proof, CTA and approving owner. Write one primary CTA for the page; keep lower-priority alternatives visually secondary.

Compare qualified responses by offer with the audience and channel as similar as possible. A higher click rate with fewer qualified replies can mean the hook attracts the wrong audience.

Say it in a meeting

“Would a restaurant owner know what they get, or are we just listing features? What part of that claim can we show today?”

Try it

Rewrite: “We’ll dominate Google Maps and double your bookings.”

Reveal model answer

Model: “See what diners find when they look up your restaurant. Get a one-location check of hours, menu and booking links.” It says what we control and gives a simple next step.

Follow the customer journey

Funnel, intent & landing pages

Intent

A journey is what a person actually does. A funnel is a way to count progress across a set of stages. Awareness is discovery, consideration is learning or comparing, and conversion is the action you defined in advance. People can loop back or skip steps.

The problem

At Juniper Harbor, Sales reports ten leads, the web analytics dashboard reports twelve conversions, and the restaurant reports eight bookings. The numbers can all be correct. They describe different people, different systems and different commitments. Calling everything conversion does not merely confuse reporting; it encourages optimization toward actions that might never produce value.

The journey diagram is the hypothesis about how a person decides. The measurement funnel is a deliberately narrower model of observables. Do not add a state simply because an analytics tool can count it. Add one when it helps the team detect a specific failure or make a specific decision.

The solution

For JML: restaurant manager sees a handout → visits the audit page → starts or submits a request → record is validated → Sales qualifies it. For Juniper Table: diner sees Maps listing → visits menu or booking → may actually dine. These are separate funnels, with different customers and outcomes.

Real-world analogy

An airport itinerary shows where a traveler may go. The gate scanner knows who boarded, not why passengers wandered or whether someone took a different route. Both views are useful when you do not confuse them.

How it works

  1. Sketch the paths for the owner and the diner separately. Write each participant’s decision in their own words before adding a box or an event.
  2. Assign each state a verb, a system of record and a trigger. A lead is accepted only when the assigned salesperson records acceptance, not when a form sends an alert.
  3. Define the unit, such as visitor ID, request ID or location, and decide how repeat visits and duplicate requests are handled.
  4. Check the route on a phone: keep one clear main action; test a wrong branch, a network failure, an accessibility obstacle and a successful submit.
  5. Add a measurement checkpoint after every consequential handoff. A funnel should reveal a wrong recipient or a dead queue instead of flattering the landing page.

Worked example

Suppose forty people start an inquiry and fifteen press submit. Twelve records reach the CRM; ten are eligible. A confusing form may explain the first drop, while a silent integration failure explains three missing records. More traffic would do nothing to repair those three losses and could make support harder.

If web data cannot be linked to CRM outcomes lawfully or reliably, compare aggregate counts using matching time and scope. Mark the unmatched slice rather than implying a clean person-level path.

When to use it

Use journey mapping to design the first handoff and funnel reporting to find where to investigate. Break steps apart when their remedies differ; merge them when the distinction changes no decision. Read M2 for promise continuity, M6 for end-to-end QA and M7 for stage reporting.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Separates demand, intake validity and sales handling so each bottleneck has the right owner.
  • Keeps a client’s diners out of an agency pipeline and avoids inflated conversions.

Limits

  • People move across devices, channels and time, so no diagram captures every path.
  • Over-instrumentation increases maintenance and privacy costs without necessarily improving decisions.

Do the work

Draw two tracks, mark where each event is captured, and identify the one next step each page should make easy. Test readability and the main button on a phone; check form success, error path, and where each lead lands.

Use a denominator aligned to the event. A QR scan is not a unique visitor, a form start isn’t a submit, and a submit isn’t a qualified lead.

Say it in a meeting

“When we say conversion, do we mean an audit request, a qualified opportunity, or a restaurant booking? Which system counts it?”

Try it

For 200 unique visitors, 16 submissions and 12 valid inquiries, calculate raw and valid visitor-to-request rates. What do the four rejected records tell you?

Reveal model answer

Model: 16/200 = 8% raw, 12/200 = 6% valid. Inspect rejection reasons before changing the marketing: missing branch details, duplicates and contact permission each suggest a different fix.

Capture local and search intent

Google Business Profile & SEO

Intent

Google Business Profile (GBP) helps people navigate a business in Maps and Search. Search engine optimization (SEO) improves the discoverability and usefulness of web pages. A profile can be accurate while the website still sends visitors to a broken menu; treat them as connected but different systems.

The problem

Juniper Harbor’s local listing tells diners the restaurant serves Sunday dinner. The restaurant’s website sends them to an archived menu that names a brunch-only Sunday schedule. The practical issue is not low rank; it is that an interested diner must resolve ambiguity before taking action. Increasing exposure first makes the friction more visible.

Separate three kinds of work. Accuracy makes public business facts reliable. Search discoverability helps relevant people find the correct page. Conversion makes a useful action possible after discovery. Each has a different owner and proof standard. A tracked listing click can confirm a handoff began, but it cannot confirm dinner was booked.

The solution

Leo verifies Harbor’s public-facing hours against the restaurant’s approved schedule. Record the branch’s listing and website URL. Check the handoff from Maps to menu and from website to booking; use no real booking or private customer data in training.

Real-world analogy

A road sign gets a driver to the street; the number on the door gets them inside. You do not replace a missing house number by buying a bigger sign.

Step by step

  1. Sample a small set of actual task searches: brand name plus hours, cuisine plus neighborhood, and booking plus location. Record the screen and context instead of treating your result as universal.
  2. Get an authoritative source for address, phone, ordinary hours, special hours and booking ownership. Use the operator’s approved facts when two public surfaces disagree.
  3. Check both paths: listing to correct branch page and branch page to accurate menu and booking. Resolve redirects and mobile dead ends before adding traffic.
  4. If a page is inaccessible to search, have the site owner check whether the correct public URL can be discovered and indexed. Name the specific problem instead of claiming search will rank it higher.
  5. Recheck the happy path after publication, preserve the evidence and flag caches or external vendors you cannot control.

Worked example

The listing opens the correct website, but the site’s booking button goes to a marketplace URL for a different branch. The listing owner does not need to edit a correct URL; the website owner needs to fix the destination and verify a dry run without making a real reservation.

If customers ask about holiday opening repeatedly, keeping special hours current is a durable operational fix. Search ranking changes on their own are not proof the fix drove revenue.

When to use it

Prioritize correctness for name, location, hours, phone and destination before ranking work when existing demand is at risk. If the basics hold, add useful pages for genuine customer questions, not near-duplicate pages manufactured around every neighborhood. M3 covers handoff design and M7 separates exposure from outcomes.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Accuracy improvements protect demand the business already has.
  • A verification trail gives the client a way to diagnose the next mismatch.

Limits

  • Search views vary by location and device; a screen capture is a sample.
  • Third-party menu and booking systems may be outside your control; document ownership rather than promising immediate correction.

Do the work

Collect timestamped screenshots and label observed vs confirmed. Backup the previous values and record who approved corrections. If approved and appropriately authorized, use role-based access rather than asking for passwords. Retest the public flow after publication and record exceptions.

Record date ranges, location and event definitions. Read Google’s profile performance and local ranking documentation; never guarantee placement or use incentivized reviews.

Say it in a meeting

“Can we fix the hours and booking link before we try to increase traffic? Which person owns special hours?”

Try it

Google’s report increased from 20 to 30 call-button clicks. What can you report? What would verify a business outcome?

Reveal model answer

Model: 10 more profile call-button clicks in the compared window. That does not establish answered calls, reservations or causation. If permitted, compare aggregate answered calls or confirmed reservations, note prior volume, date range and other changes.

Choose channels on purpose

Content, social, paid, email & partners

Intent

Owned channels are assets you directly control, like a website. Organic distribution relies on unpaid reach; paid distribution buys placement. Inbound brings an interested buyer to you, while outbound initiates a conversation. Pick based on buyer intent, available proof, cost and follow-up capacity, not what is fashionable.

The problem

Juniper Harbor could add a creator partnership, a paid search campaign and an email series. The same two people would be on the hook for approvals, reply coverage and sales follow-up. A sensible channel strategy starts with the slot you have for a buyer, not the accounts you can technically open.

Distinguish a distribution channel, a destination and a proof asset. A handout or partner introduction creates attention. A landing page captures an action. A post or case example can prove the claim in either route. Counting these as three parallel growth bets hides how they work together and makes staffing look cheaper than it is.

The options

JML uses an approved handout to start owner conversations, an audit landing page to capture consent, and one practical local-search post as proof of expertise. Juniper Table can separately test a clear menu link and existing opted-in subscriber updates. Those subscribers are not JML leads.

Side by side

Search-led discovery

Primary when prospects are looking now; high intent can expose a broken location page immediately.

Proof-led distribution

Useful when prospects need to see competence before they trust the offer; requires follow-up capacity.

Real-world analogy

A restaurant can open a second entrance, but that does not increase the number of tables or the number of servers. Add an entrance only if the dining room can handle the arrivals.

How it works

  1. Describe the buying moment: urgent fix, planned review, referral, or early awareness. Choose a channel that reaches the buyer in that moment.
  2. Rank candidates by buyer fit, credibility, cost, staffing load and measurement. Reject a channel if it relies on permission or evidence you do not have.
  3. Allocate staff hours to response and sales first; work backward to a feasible volume. Reserve a portion of capacity for unexpected questions.
  4. Pair a primary distribution route with an owned destination and one proof asset. Specify a qualifying event, follow-up owner and stop condition.
  5. Set a review date. Continue a channel that produces useful qualified actions under the cost cap, iterate a fixable bottleneck, and pause when the team cannot keep its response promise.

Worked example

An illustrative comparison: a local restaurant partner offers two introductions but needs 90 minutes of coordination; a brief visit route takes four hours and starts five owner conversations. If calendars can handle only three audit appointments, neither route is superior on volume alone. Compare fit, expected scheduling, the actual staff hours and whether the introduction reached an approver.

An organic post may be worth keeping as proof even if it did not create an independent lead stream. Judge it on the job it was assigned, not on the expectations of paid demand capture.

How to choose

Use direct or referral routes when few qualified conversations would teach more than wide exposure. Use paid channels after offer, tracking and follow-up have held up at small scale. M1 supplies buyer fit, M2 proof and M6 release readiness.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Protects team capacity and keeps attribution roles distinct.
  • Lets a proof asset do useful work across channels without treating reuse as a new campaign.

Limits

  • Partner and organic tests can yield sparse early samples.
  • Some effective routes, especially referrals, are hard to attribute cleanly; allow for self-reported or unknown sources.

Do the work

Make a channel one-pager: audience, intent, asset, CTA, owner, event, budget if applicable and stop rule. Organic: one helpful before/after example with permission. Paid: preapprove spend and test complete tracking first. Email: use only allowed contacts, honor opt-outs, and don't count opens as bookings.

Compare qualified inquiries per approved dollar and staff hour, using the same buyer definition. Include production time when deciding whether “free” distribution is efficient.

Say it in a meeting

“Which channel gets us in front of the decision maker at the right time? Do we have capacity to follow up if the test works?”

Try it

Choose two channels for JML when the team can handle only five discovery calls next week.

Reveal model answer

Model: choose a scoped field visit and the landing-page capture path. Put paid scale on hold until staffing and measurement work. Pick organic as a supporting asset if it won’t absorb the time needed for follow-up.

Launch without losing the lead

Creative brief, tracking & quality checks

Intent

A campaign brief joins audience, objective, message, asset, decision and measurement. UTM tags label campaign traffic; they are not attribution proof. A version identifies which offer, creative and destination produced a captured event.

The problem

A Juniper Harbor handout is approved, the QR opens the audit page, and the page reports success. Yet Sales receives a duplicate with neither an assigned owner nor the correct offer version. The creative was completed, but the campaign was not operational. Release readiness covers what happens after the public asset works.

Treat the brief as a contract between strategy, production, review and the receiving team. Each group needs a different output: a claim and purpose, an exact asset specification, a testable gate, and a lead that Sales can act on. If the brief does not name the acceptance event, the handoff will be declared complete too early.

The solution

Create a QR handout to the approved audit page. Practice URL: https://example.com/audit?utm_source=field_handout&utm_medium=qr&utm_campaign=restaurant_gbp&utm_content=audit_v1. CRM stores the source, offer version, location, timestamp, contact permission, requested help and owner.

Real-world analogy

Running a relay is more than watching each runner move fast. The team also needs a clean baton handoff, inside the correct lane, before the next runner can start.

Structure and workflow

  1. List the approved claim, destination ID, named approver, asset version and responsible CRM owner before production starts.
  2. Define a test sheet with expected results for a normal phone visit, duplicate submission, bad form values, a down queue and an operator attempting the wrong branch.
  3. Use a fictitious record and check every field needed to validate it, including branch, request time and consent signal. Confirm tags survive redirects where your architecture supports them.
  4. Validate role routing by having the intended owner open the record, confirm accept/reject reasons and verify the follow-up timer. Check that users outside the brand assignment cannot see it.
  5. Set a recovery rule. If intake fails, pause the affected asset or use an already approved alternative path that still records required fields. Fix, rerun, obtain sign-off and archive the test result.

Worked example

Suppose a printed batch includes the older code after the landing route changes. The earlier route still loads but drops the campaign label. Keep QR links stable where possible, compare the redirect’s final destination with the version register, and have the authorized owner repair the redirect or retire that print batch.

Separately, if two testing submissions share an email but represent different branches, deduping by email alone may lose a legitimate request. Test the exact tradeoff with approved fake records.

When to use it

Use full end-to-end checks when a change touches claims, permissions, destination, lead fields or routing. For a typo-only change, scope the test to the differences and the main path. M2 supplies accepted claims, M3 defines state transitions and M7 sets the reporting units.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Catches silent failures before physical distribution or paid spend.
  • Creates a reproducible record for recovery next time.

Limits

  • Testing can consume a chunk of a small release window; prioritize by impact instead of testing every permutation.
  • A good dry run does not guarantee future availability; have a real monitoring owner and pause rule.

Do the work

Do a dry run on mobile: open the asset, scan the QR, verify the URL and labels, submit an approved test record with no real personal data, verify its route and dedupe behavior, and remove or mark the test record per policy.

Track end-to-end test pass rate and validated inquiries divided by submissions. Handoff is complete when Sales can see and accept the record, not when the form says “thank you.”

Say it in a meeting

“Who signs off on the claim, who tests the form, and where do hand-raises go if the queue is down?”

Try it

A campaign was approved, but test leads have no consent field in CRM. What would you do?

Reveal model answer

Model: pause release or keep traffic off the broken capture path. Ask the owner to fix and retest the required field all the way through. Do not infer consent or compensate by collecting extra data.

M7 · Skill · 6 MIN + PRACTICE

Measure what moves the business

Analytics, experiments & reporting

Intent

A KPI is a measure chosen to judge a goal. A leading indicator moves earlier; a lagging indicator is closer to the eventual outcome. Attribution assigns observed credit under a model, not proof that a channel caused the outcome. A useful test names one intended change, primary metric and a guardrail before it starts.

The problem

A Juniper Harbor report says inquiries doubled. The next question is not “how fast can we spend more?” It is “which inquiries, from when, and how far did they get?” If this week’s cohort has not had time to make a decision, comparing its wins with last month’s completed cohort rewards early activity and punishes slower deals.

Keep three ideas separate: operational reporting explains what was recorded, attribution rules assign observed credit and a controlled comparison estimates what changed because of a treatment. A model can split credit across touchpoints, but that is not the same as observing what those buyers would have done otherwise.

The solution

Fictional weekly figures: 240 visits → 24 submissions → 18 valid → 6 discoveries → 2 wins; direct campaign spend $360. Cost per valid inquiry is $20. Simple spend per win is $180; that excludes labor and is not fully loaded customer acquisition cost.

Real-world analogy

A sales ledger tells you which receipts were recorded. It cannot tell you whether last Saturday’s chalkboard sign made the difference unless you had a sensible comparison.

Step by step

  1. Pick one decision first, such as maintain, change or pause a pilot. Name one primary outcome and one guardrail before you look at the result.
  2. Define the cohort with dates, timezone, branch, eligibility, source rules and observation window. Make sure outcomes had roughly equal time to mature.
  3. Compare counts and rates together, and include time or cost that changed. Keep paid and organic exposure separate whenever the source supports it.
  4. Check alternative explanations: software changed, a holiday shifted demand, the team responded later, or attribution coverage changed. Mark small samples and missing data plainly.
  5. Log the decision, reason, owner and next review. If the evidence only identifies a bottleneck, run the smallest credible follow-up test rather than writing a confident causal headline.

Worked example

In an illustrative comparison, two four-week cohorts each have ten eligible inquiries. The first produced four discovery calls and the second produced two. If six second-cohort inquiries entered in the final two days and the ordinary booking delay is a week, do not interpret two versus four as proof of falling quality. Compare equally mature outcomes and inspect response time now.

If a tracked landing page shows better results but a simultaneous discount went live, say what moved and what remains confounded. Consider a comparable holdout or staggered rollout before attributing the lift.

When to use it

Use weekly reporting to remove operational bottlenecks, a mature cohort to make budget decisions, and an experiment when the proposed change is consequential enough to justify one. Revisit M3 when stages are unclear, M5 for effort tradeoffs and M6 for missing tracking or dropped records.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Produces evidence that leads to a concrete action, not a vanity dashboard.
  • Makes cost, time and incomplete data visible before someone scales.

Limits

  • A fair comparison can require more time than a weekly slide allows.
  • A rate based on small or unmatched groups can swing sharply without a meaningful underlying change.

Do the work

Report one outcome and one blocker. Define cohort, date range, sources and missing segments. Compare like periods, check holidays and changes, and write one recommended action with owner and date. For low volume, call the finding directional and do not claim statistical certainty.

Use campaign source plus CRM stage outcomes. Distinguish revenue from gross profit, recorded bookings and channel clicks. Always label training numbers as fictional.

Say it in a meeting

“Valid inquiries are up but discoveries are flat. Is the issue lead quality, rep pickup time, or our measurement?”

Try it

Calculate the valid visitor rate and discovery rate among valid inquiries. A proposed ad raises traffic but lowers the valid rate. What would you check next?

Reveal model answer

Model: 18/240 = 7.5%; 6/18 ≈ 33%. Compare total valid volume, qualified outcomes and cost at the same time as rate. Confirm consistent definitions, audience and cohort before choosing a winner.

Read the pipeline

Lead, account, contact & opportunity

Intent

An account is a business; a contact is a person at that business. A lead is someone to evaluate, not a sale. An opportunity is a possible sale that meets your stage rules. A CRM is the shared record. A pipeline shows where individual deals stand; a funnel describes counted progression through stages.

The problem

On Monday the Harbor location’s manager scans the flyer and leaves an inquiry. On Tuesday the owner of Garden, a second Juniper location, replies to a community event invitation. Both messages include “Juniper” and both arrive in the same week, but neither gives JML permission to combine the branches or treat two unrelated conversations as one sale.

The purpose of the record is to let another person pick up the work without making up a story. Imagine a colleague waking up to a board showing one large “Juniper” deal in Proposal. They might ask both locations for access or promise work to the wrong buyer. The correction is to preserve the relationship between the account, its locations, the people, and each distinct buying decision.

The solution

Juniper Table is the account, Maya and Leo are contacts, and JML‑104 is a validated inquiry for Harbor. Maya’s approval authority and the actual branch problem still need to be confirmed. Garden Branch is not automatically part of this deal.

Real-world analogy

Think of a hotel reservation system: a guest profile describes a person, the hotel is a location, and a reservation is a specific transaction. A frequent guest asking what dates are available isn’t the same as a confirmed stay.

How it works

  1. Start with the legal or trading name and the location’s address or public site. Search probable duplicate names, then check location and contact before linking.
  2. Create one inquiry record for the Harbor request. Link the person and the branch, then note why the other Juniper conversation is separate.
  3. Advance the stage only after evidence: a held conversation isn’t a qualified opportunity until the agreed criteria are met.
  4. Record the next observable action, who owns it and its date. Leave open amounts blank or explicitly estimated until a scope supports a value.

Worked example

At Harbor, JML has an appointment and one verified listing discrepancy. Keep the inquiry as Discovery scheduled while approval authority and recurring need are unknown. At Garden, the event reply may simply be a marketing interaction. If the Harbor owner later wants ongoing multi-location work, open or update the opportunity only after confirming whether there’s one buying decision or two separate approvals.

When to use it

Use the pipeline for management decisions about individual records and the funnel to compare progression across a defined group. Counting inquiry records instead of distinct accounts may be appropriate for measuring support workload, but it can inflate new-business reach. Agree on the counting unit before comparing reports.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Creates an evidence trail for each stage, even when several people work the same account.
  • Retains branch differences and makes sales handoffs safer.

Limits

  • A detailed CRM design is burdensome if reps are asked to fill fields no one uses.
  • Rigid gates can be misleading when buyers skip steps; record evidence, not just boxes.

Do the work

Find or create the correct account and location only after checking for duplicates. Log the inquiry separately from any opportunity. Use stage gates: accepted intake, discovery held, qualified opportunity, scoped proposal and explicit decision.

Pipeline value is potential, not contracted revenue or cash. Separate open, won, lost, disqualified and nurture records.

Say it in a meeting

“Which stage is this in, and what buyer action earned that stage? Is there a named next step and date?”

Try it

A manager scanned the flyer, asked for a quote and agreed to a meeting. What belongs in CRM?

Reveal model answer

Model: an accepted inquiry with source, correct contact and scheduled discovery. The manager’s question is genuine interest, not proof of authority, fit or a won deal.

S2 · Comparison · 5 MIN + PRACTICE

Prepare and open well

Inbound follow-up & field outreach

Intent

Inbound starts with a buyer raising a hand; outbound starts when a rep initiates contact. Both need relevance and respect for permission. Your first goal is a useful conversation, not to deliver the whole pitch.

The problem

Juniper Harbor receives a dozen vague “we can grow your restaurant” pitches each month. A busy manager has no incentive to listen to one more. The rep’s challenge is not a lack of talking points. It is earning attention without imposing on service or jumping from an observation to a diagnosis.

Compare two openings. The first says, “Your restaurant is losing customers because your hours are wrong.” That overstates what can be observed. The second points to two dated public pages, doesn’t assume either one is correct, and asks permission to show the difference. It offers a specific benefit and leaves the manager free to decline.

The options

At Harbor Branch, a dated public check finds Google says 8 p.m. on Sunday and the website says 9 p.m. Say: “Hi, I’m with JML. Is now a bad time? I noticed two pages show different Sunday hours for this location. Would it help if I show you what a guest sees?”

Side by side

Inbound

A buyer initiated a specific request. Acknowledge the original need and use the channel they agreed to.

Outbound

You initiate. Show one relevant public observation and earn permission to continue.

Real-world analogy

Treat the first touch like knocking on a neighbor’s door, not walking into their kitchen. You can explain why you came and ask whether now works. The other person decides what happens next.

How it works

  1. Write down why this exact location is relevant today. A generic compliment or template does not establish relevance.
  2. Prepare one brief fact, one low-friction question, one useful next step, and an easy way to say no.
  3. Separate evidence from inference: hours differ is observed; lost sales is a hypothesis. Ask which source reflects the real schedule.
  4. Record the contact channel and any express preference. If someone declines, don’t repurpose a public email to get around their response.

Worked example

Harbor emails for a price after seeing the flyer. A short reply could say, “Thanks for asking. Is this for Harbor only, and are you mainly trying to fix listings or keep several channels aligned?” Garden has not reached out, so an approved in-person visit has a different opening and nothing can be inferred from Harbor’s permission. If the manager prefers next week, record the agreed day and channel instead of adding the contact to a generic campaign.

How to choose

Compare two message openings only when the audience, period, and contact method are comparable. Count conversations by a consistent definition: a meaningful answer or agreed next step is not the same as a voicemail or auto-reply. Keep an opt-out or clear no separate from “no response.”

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Specific evidence helps a buyer decide quickly whether a conversation is useful.
  • Recording preferences prevents avoidable repeat contacts and improves handoffs.

Limits

  • Public information can be stale, so an apparent discrepancy may not reflect the real problem.
  • Personalizing every touch takes time; qualify which accounts warrant it.

Do the work

Before visiting, confirm branch and public links, choose a quieter window, prepare one screenshot, the approved audit asset and a clear exit. For inbound, confirm the approved reply channel and request. If the person is busy, ask if they prefer a later time or no follow-up, and honor the answer.

Reply rate = distinct accounts that replied ÷ distinct accounts contacted in the same cohort. Multiple attempts don’t create more accounts.

Say it in a meeting

“Are we measuring accounts contacted or attempts? Can we see which opening actually earned a conversation?”

Try it

The owner says “Not now, we’re in service.” Give an answer without pitching again.

Reveal model answer

Model: “Of course. Is there a better time, or would you prefer I leave it here?” If they decline, log the outcome and don’t invent follow-up permission.

S3 · Skill · 5 MIN + PRACTICE

Diagnose before proposing

Discovery, qualification & recap

Intent

Discovery makes the problem and buying process explicit. Qualification asks whether the problem, ability to help, timing and people line up. Some good meetings end with no deal, or with a recommendation to fix it internally.

The problem

When Harbor staff hear that listings might need help, they first think of typing different hours into a page. Discovery separates the visible symptom from the work underneath. Maybe the hours differ because Leo fixes holiday exceptions on Google while someone else owns the website. A one-time correction helps today; no one has yet taken responsibility for keeping them aligned next month.

The goal isn’t to ask questions until the buyer agrees to the service JML wants to sell. It’s to find the smallest credible solution and understand who can choose it. The buyer may already have a tool or an employee who can fix the problem, with a specialist needed only for the initial cleanup.

The solution

Ask Maya: “What does a diner do if they want to visit on Sunday?” Ask Leo: “Who changes special hours and handles holiday exceptions?” Ask both: “What would tell us this is fixed, and who would need to approve changes or a budget?”

Real-world analogy

A misaligned door can come from a loose hinge or a warped frame. Before replacing the door, check which part actually causes the problem.

Step by step

  1. Ask how the current workflow works, and have the buyer walk through one recent example from first signal to resolution.
  2. Identify the effect and evidence. If the team can’t quantify it, agree what log or count would make it verifiable.
  3. Confirm the decision path: who owns the problem, who can approve an update, who approves budget, and what date matters to them.
  4. Recap in the buyer’s language. Mark what is verified, observed, or still unknown and ask them to correct the record.

Worked example

Leo says a holiday-hours mismatch took forty minutes across two pages, but there’s no record of lost bookings. Maya is the decision owner and wants the next long weekend handled. An appropriately scoped recap is: “We verified the public discrepancy and learned updates are split between owners. Can Leo confirm who owns the website before our Thursday review? We’ll compare a one-time fix with an ongoing maintenance option.”

When to use it

A fact can be technically correct and still not be useful: once the issue was corrected internally and the buyer has a reliable process, there may be no reason for a proposal. Keep the record in nurture only if the buyer wants a later check-in. Ask whether lack of fit or lack of urgency explains a pause.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • A shared recap reduces scope drift and unnecessary proposals.
  • Discovery gives buyers a credible do-it-yourself path where appropriate.

Limits

  • Early conversation depends on what attendees know; ask for the actual owner when facts are uncertain.
  • An important problem is not always an available budget or signed decision.

Do the work

Agree on agenda, ask open questions, replay the problem in their words, and confirm one next step. Mark evidence as observed, verified or unknown. If only one correction is needed, say so. Ask about the buyer's timeline rather than creating false urgency.

Discovery held ÷ valid inquiries and qualified opportunities ÷ discoveries held answer different questions. Log disqualification reasons without speculation.

Say it in a meeting

“Is this an isolated mismatch or an ongoing workflow issue? Who can verify the branch details and make the decision?”

Try it

Maya asks, “Why can’t I just change the hours myself?” Answer in a way that leaves a real choice.

Reveal model answer

Model: “You can. If it’s a one-off, correct it internally and check both pages. If keeping hours, menu and bookings in sync is a recurring problem, we can scope the ongoing work.”

S4 · Pattern · 5 MIN + PRACTICE

Keep one accountable record

Intake gates, duplicate handling & meeting hygiene

Intent

An assignment sends work to someone; acceptance makes it that person’s responsibility. A lead-sorting rule should not overwrite records if identity is ambiguous. A next action needs an owner, date and outcome.

The problem

Two forms reach the Sales queue about Harbor. One uses the phrase “Juniper Harbor,” the other gives the parent business name and street address. Duplicate intake can accidentally trigger two calls, two owners, or an inflated report. The opposite mistake is worse: merging Harbor and Garden can change the wrong account or disclose notes to the wrong team.

Good record hygiene preserves ambiguity rather than pretending the system knows more than it does. A matching rule can suggest records, but it cannot override contradictory location details. If no one accepts the record, the queue is still responsible for getting it to a named human by a definite time.

The solution

JML‑104 checks for an exact account and Harbor match. If Garden is the only match, send it to human review; don’t merge it based on restaurant name alone. Existing confirmed match: link and update. New clear location: assign. Ambiguous: hold with owner/time.

Real-world analogy

At a coat check, the claim ticket points to one coat. A familiar-looking jacket does not prove it belongs to the same person. When the labels disagree, stop and verify instead of handing it over.

Structure and workflow

  1. Compare identifiers before merging: exact location and confirmed account relationship matter more than similar names.
  2. Put conflicting records on hold with an owner, review deadline and reason. Preserve the original submissions until the responsible reviewer decides.
  3. When a match is confirmed, link the new evidence to the correct record rather than overwriting old evidence without a trace.
  4. After a meeting, document the decision, relevant buyer roles and next action. Keep unsupported commentary and private information out of the notes.

Worked example

An email from Maya references Harbor and uses the same address as an open draft. A teammate temporarily labeled it Garden. A reviewer checks the branch context with the approved contact, records that the branch was corrected, and updates the existing inquiry. In the recap, Maya’s casual “looks good” is logged as a positive signal, while an owner remains responsible for checking the actual proposal decision date.

When to use it

Use automated rules for reliable identifiers, such as an exact branch ID, rather than for lookalike names. Audit the held queue to measure how often the rules defer legitimate records. A slow human review may delay an outreach, but a careless merge can leak the wrong context and create more correction work.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • One accountable owner and backup make follow-through observable.
  • Clear records simplify both reporting and correction when something goes wrong.

Limits

  • Manual review takes capacity, particularly when incoming records are incomplete.
  • Overzealous deduplication can suppress legitimate separate business opportunities.

Do the work

Record: account, branch, contact role, approved channel, time, source/version, observed evidence, stage, blocker, action, owner and date. Use buyer behavior as the stage gate. Don’t store credentials, card numbers or unrelated guest information.

Track time from intake to acceptance and stale records without a next action. If a queue item is past its due date, route it through the designated backup path.

Say it in a meeting

“Harbor discovery is booked for Tuesday. Leo verifies the hours, Maya approves, and I’ll bring a one-location scope option. Garden isn’t in scope.”

Try it

Sales sent a proposal. Maya said “looks good,” but hasn’t signed or accepted through the agreed process. What is the correct stage?

Reveal model answer

Model: proposal sent / decision pending. Record the positive signal and agree the next decision date; don’t mark it won or report billed revenue.

Scope the work and answer objections

Proposal, pricing & commercial judgment

Intent

A proposal turns verified needs into written deliverables and exclusions. Objections may reflect missing evidence, risk, timing, money or unclear authority. Clarify which risk concerns the buyer rather than answering every objection with a discount.

The problem

Harbor is considering help before a crowded holiday weekend. Sales wants to present something easy to accept, and the buyer wants to know whether it solves the actual risk. A vague “local growth” plan makes both sides fill in the blanks. The buyer may assume it includes changes to their website, while Delivery assumes it covers the listing only.

Write the proposal around work the team controls and decisions the buyer must make. If Maya asks for cheaper pricing, it could mean her budget is capped, she is comparing scopes, or she doubts ongoing work is necessary. The right response depends on which uncertainty she’s trying to reduce. Discounting before finding out can preserve the misunderstanding.

The solution

A proposal for Harbor could include approved hours/menu/link updates, one documented baseline and a review cadence, with website rebuilds, rankings, paid media and the second branch excluded. Use current, approved commercial terms; never rely on a training example as a quote.

Real-world analogy

A tailored quote works like a labeled floor plan. Everyone can see which rooms are included, which walls stay untouched, and what must be moved before the work begins.

Step by step

  1. Describe the verified problem and the outcome the work is meant to support, without promising an outcome you cannot control.
  2. Set the boundary: one location, named channels, measurable deliverables, timing and assumptions. List common exclusions buyers might otherwise expect.
  3. Ask what the objection means. If the buyer needs a decision next week, find out what event or constraint makes that date important.
  4. Send commercial exceptions to the authorized owner and note the exact question. Update the dated proposal only after the revised terms are confirmed.

Worked example

Maya says, “Another firm offered it cheaper.” Rather than offer a discount, ask whether they’re comparing a one-time correction or ongoing monitoring. A smaller scope might meet her need: update the agreed listing fields, record the baseline, and stop after verification. If she wants new pages, reporting, and a promise about Maps ranking, separate what can be scoped from what cannot be guaranteed.

When to use it

If multiple versions circulate, reference the specific date or version in the acceptance. Keep time-sensitive terms and cancellation conditions where the buyer can find them. When evaluating objections, record enough context to improve future proposals without putting subjective assessments of the buyer in the CRM.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Clear inclusions and exclusions protect expectation and delivery quality.
  • A smaller, honestly scoped offer can be more useful than a broad package.

Limits

  • An overly restrictive scope can leave an important dependency unaddressed.
  • Custom terms and promises add review time and can delay a legitimate purchase.

Do the work

Write scope, location, units or cadence, access, approval, timeline and dependencies, measurement, approved price and billing terms, cancellation terms and a dated decision step. If unsure, log the exact question and ask the authorized owner to confirm in writing.

Track decided proposal win rate with both counts. Proposals that are still open don’t belong in the resolved denominator.

Say it in a meeting

“We can commit to the listed work and reporting. We can’t promise a specific Maps rank or reservation count. What evidence would help you evaluate the scope?”

Try it

Maya asks for a discount, a start this Friday and a guarantee. Reply without making three unauthorized commitments.

Reveal model answer

Model: “We don’t guarantee rankings. I’ll confirm approved pricing and availability in writing. Is Friday tied to an event we should plan around?” Open a manager review for scope, discounts, refunds or terms.

Close, hand off and retain

Decision, kickoff, reporting & feedback

Intent

A close records a buyer’s decision and any conditions. Delivery starts after its lead accepts the handoff, not merely when Sales sends a message. Retention comes from fulfilling and explaining agreed value, not adding unapproved work.

The problem

A buyer’s acceptance feels like the end of Sales, but it is the beginning of the client’s real experience. Imagine Maya signs on Friday and sends a note about the holiday menu. On Monday, Delivery has a proposal PDF, no confirmed access, and no idea who can approve the menu link. The client thought work was already underway. The handoff failed because the next owner never accepted it.

Put the accepted decision, permissions and an actionable first milestone in one place. Keep the person who sold the work available to clarify the promises, but don’t make Delivery rummage through sales chat to reconstruct the scope.

The solution

When an approved scope is accepted, send Delivery the signed version, Harbor’s URLs, verified facts, baseline, open risk, customer approver, role-based access needed, permissions and milestone. If there is no decision, document why and any allowed check-in date.

Real-world analogy

Passing the baton in a relay is complete only when the next runner has it in hand. The person who releases their grip first has not necessarily made a successful handoff.

Structure and workflow

  1. Record the decision and the exact accepted scope, including any conditions before kickoff, such as role-based access or approval of public copy.
  2. Give Delivery the latest validated facts, baseline, key contacts, review date and unresolved questions. State which location or channels are excluded.
  3. Have the receiving lead accept ownership and confirm the first dependency. If no lead is assigned, Sales keeps the follow-up visible instead of calling the transfer complete.
  4. At review, distinguish work done, observed evidence, plausible explanations, and data gaps. Confirm the next decision and its owner.

Worked example

Delivery verifies Harbor’s listing hours and menu link; the baseline shows 40 tracked clicks and the next period 55, but there is no reservation reconciliation. Report 15 more tracked clicks for that comparison and explain that clicks are not tables booked. If an authorized aggregate reservation export becomes available, check the time window and tracking definitions before making any attribution claim.

When to use it

A client asks to add Garden in the first review. Treat this as a scope question rather than an implied commitment. Clarify the decision owner and whether a separate estimate is required. When a client is satisfied, preserve the approved results and still avoid sharing confidential data as a case study without authorization.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • A verified handoff reduces repeated questions and duplicated work.
  • Precise reporting earns trust even when data is incomplete.

Limits

  • A checklist must not substitute for a receiving owner who actually accepts.
  • Attribution gaps limit the strength of performance claims; don’t fill them with estimates presented as facts.

Do the work

Use a handoff gate: exact scope and terms current; approvals confirmed; access invited only by authorized owner, no shared passwords; open tasks assigned; first review date noted; Delivery accepts. At review, report changes and evidence separately from unresolved attribution.

Measure win-to-kickoff time, customer decisions and reporting quality. Send only approved, appropriately scoped outcome insights back to Marketing.

Say it in a meeting

“Delivery accepted Harbor. The first action is to verify Sunday hours and retest the menu link after approval. There’s no evidence yet on completed bookings.”

Try it

What do you tell a client when the listing shows more clicks but they ask how many tables JML added?

Reveal model answer

Model: report the tracked clicks and their limits. Ask for aggregate confirmed bookings or a provider export if authorized. Don’t imply credit if the channel can’t be reconciled.

S7 · Skill · 6 MIN + PRACTICE

Forecast and speak up in meetings

Metrics, judgment & escalation

Intent

A forecast estimates likely future outcomes under defined assumptions; it is not a promise. A useful meeting update names stage, evidence, risk, decision needed and next action. A short question about definitions can make you more useful than a confident guess.

The problem

During a Friday review, a team sees two proposals from twenty contacted accounts and one acceptance. Someone wants to set next month’s revenue target using a “50% close rate.” That phrasing hides the undecided proposal and converts a tiny historic cohort into confidence it cannot support.

The purpose of forecasting is to surface assumptions and help decide which action would change the outcome. A stage can only help if it’s used consistently. Proposals pending beyond their expected decision dates aren’t equivalent to newly issued proposals. A useful forecast points to the eventual buyer decisions rather than multiplying an undifferentiated pipeline value.

The solution

Example cohort: 20 distinct businesses contacted, 5 replied, 3 held discovery, 2 received proposals. One is won and one is open. Report 25% reply rate, 15% discovery per contacted, one win and one open proposal. A decided proposal rate of 1/1 is technically correct and far too small to forecast reliably.

Real-world analogy

A weather forecast states the chance of rain under current conditions. It doesn’t promise the rain will happen or treat yesterday’s weather as tomorrow’s result. A sales forecast should also explain its conditions.

Step by step

  1. Agree on the reporting unit and period before computing rates. Distinguish accounts, inquiry records, proposals, wins, signed value and collected cash.
  2. Group open opportunities by stage and likely decision period. Note uncertain timing, unverified evidence, and meaningful dependencies.
  3. In the meeting, share one observation and one proposed move. Ask a concise clarification if the number would otherwise lead to the wrong action.
  4. After the meeting, record the owner and dated next step. Update the forecast when evidence changes rather than preserving an obsolete estimate.

Worked example

Suppose Harbor is the one win, and a second scope is undecided because the decision owner has not reviewed it. The update is: “Twenty contacted, five replies, three discoveries, two proposals. One won; one still open. I’ll confirm whether next Tuesday is a real decision date before counting it in the near-term forecast.” You can use a clearly labeled scenario: if the second proposal is accepted, show the incremental signed value; if not, show the lower case without inventing a reason.

When to use it

Use metrics to decide where to inspect, not whom to blame. A low reply rate might indicate contact method or list quality; a high inquiry count with few qualified opportunities might reflect fit or the stage gate. Escalate unresolved commercial or permission risks before putting those assumptions into a public or client-facing claim.

Tradeoffs

Strengths

  • Shows what the team knows and what could change the estimate.
  • Makes the next management decision easier to see and verify.

Limits

  • Small or immature cohorts produce unstable rates.
  • Heavy scoring can create false precision when evidence of stage quality is weak.

Do the work

Before a meeting, read the record and prepare a 30-second update. During it, separate observed fact and your interpretation. After it, log decision, owner and date. Escalate terms, billing, permissions, client privacy, unapproved public claims and commitments promptly.

Know which payout or billing system is authoritative when asked about cash. In training, do not send messages, change a profile or accept terms.

Say it in a meeting

“From 20 contacted accounts, we have one win and one undecided proposal. Can we verify how we classify the open deal before using this in a forecast?”

Try it

Someone says “we closed 50%” based on this cohort. Correct constructively.

Reveal model answer

Model: “One of two proposals has become a win so far; the other hasn’t been decided. I’d show both counts rather than infer a win rate from one decision.”

Glossary

A/B test
A comparison where one controlled difference is tested across comparable groups; prioritize a clear decision and enough data.
Account / contact
A company or organization, and an individual who works with or for it.
Attribution
How a reporting system assigns credit. It cannot establish causation on its own.
Baseline
A defined starting measurement to compare with later observations.
Buyer persona
The individual role, goals, objections and constraints involved in deciding.
CAC
Customer acquisition cost: acquisition costs included by definition ÷ new customers from the relevant cohort.
Campaign / channel
A time-bounded effort with a goal, and the route through which you reach people.
Conversion / conversion rate
A named action, and the count of that action divided by an explicitly eligible group.
CPL / CPQL
Cost per lead or per qualified lead; define the lead gate and the costs included.
CRM
Customer relationship management system: the team’s source for accounts, conversations and next steps.
CTR
Click-through rate: tracked clicks ÷ impressions, using consistent definitions.
CTA
Call to action: the next action you ask the reader or buyer to take.
Discovery
A conversation to establish the issue, impact, fit and decision process.
Funnel / pipeline
Counts of progression across defined stages, versus the lineup of individually owned sales opportunities.
GBP
Google Business Profile: the public listing and management tools for eligible local businesses.
Guardrail
A constraint or metric you do not want an experiment to damage.
ICP
Ideal customer profile: the type of account or customer the solution fits best.
Inbound / outbound
The buyer initiates the interaction, or the seller initiates contact.
Lead / opportunity
A potential contact to evaluate, versus a possible deal that satisfies defined qualification criteria.
Impressions / reach
Times shown, versus distinct people or accounts under a platform’s definition.
KPI
Key performance indicator: a chosen measure used to make a decision.
MQL / SQL
Marketing-qualified lead and sales-qualified lead. Agree what each gate means locally; the labels alone prove nothing.
Positioning
How a buyer should understand the offer relative to the alternatives they would consider.
ROAS
Return on ad spend: revenue attributed to advertising ÷ ad spend. Not profit or causal impact.
SEO
Search engine optimization: making web content more useful and easier to discover via unpaid search.
Stage gate
Evidence that must exist before moving a record to another stage.
UTM
A URL label that describes a link’s source, medium or campaign; not proof of the full value chain.
Win rate
Won decisions divided by an explicitly defined decided cohort. Show counts and exclusions.

For platform rules and definitions, start with Google’s local ranking guidance, Business Profile performance, Google’s SEO Starter Guide, Google’s campaign URL builder and Salesforce’s pipeline explainer. The exercises teach an operating model, not vendor policy or a live JML offer.

Book / Capstone
JML FIELD STUDY

Capstone

Maya wants to make weeknight reservations easier. Google and the site show different Sunday hours. A handout campaign drew 240 page visits, 24 submissions and 18 valid inquiries for JML; 6 discoveries and 2 wins are documented from that illustrative cohort. Juniper reports 16 confirmed reservations over six comparable dinner services. You do not know the actual Sunday hours, or whether individual reservations came from Google.

  1. Audit: Write a one-page Harbor audit with one observation, one unknown, one proposed fix and a verification step.
  2. Plan: Pick one offer, one CTA, one channel, a consent/CRM quality gate and a business outcome. Distinguish the JML campaign objective from Juniper’s.
  3. Role-play: Take a five-minute discovery call. Ask about decision authority and timing. Respond to “Will this get us to the top of Google?” without a promise.
  4. Deliver: Log the stage, next action, owner and date, then write a 30-second status update and an example handoff.
Reveal model submission and rubric

Audit: Maps displays 8 p.m.; site displays 9 p.m.; Leo must verify actual Harbor hours and Maya approve any correction. Retest both public paths on mobile after the responsible person updates them. JML CTA: request the one-location audit; count valid, permissioned inquiries and discoveries in CRM. Juniper outcome: completed reservations per eligible dinner service (baseline 16 ÷ 6 ≈ 2.7); compare with an equivalent period and don’t claim causation without better evidence.

Role-play: “We can improve the items we can verify and report what happens; we can’t guarantee rank. Who approves changes and how will we judge whether the booking path is clearer?” Log discovery held; approval and actual hours pending. Example next step: Leo verifies Tuesday, Sales sends a Harbor-only scope Wednesday, Maya owns approval, Delivery acknowledges before any changes.

Score 0–2 each: evidence, actionability, meeting clarity, and judgment. Pass at 6/8 or higher only if there are no invented claims, unauthorized commitments, permission failures or sensitive-data leaks. Have a mentor review the first live use.
